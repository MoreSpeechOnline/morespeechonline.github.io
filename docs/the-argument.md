## Nature of this document

This is an advocacy memorandum written from the position of a federal prosecutor. It states the strongest case the law will bear for treating viewpoint-based blocking by federal officials as both a civil-rights violation and a federal crime, and it states the weaknesses in that case at full strength rather than burying them. It does not allege that any identified person has committed the conduct described. The analysis is conduct-based: it defines a category of conduct, identifies the legal consequences that attach, and specifies what would have to be proved.

Three conventions are used throughout. **[OBSERVED]** marks a proposition taken directly from the retrieved text of a statute, regulation, or judicial opinion, with the source identified. **[REPORTED]** marks a proposition taken from a decision whose full text is not stored in the research corpus — the citation is given so that the passage can be checked against the reporter before filing, and readers should assume nothing more than that until they have done so. **[INFERENCE]** marks the author's reading, application, or prediction. Quotations are verbatim from the sources cited. Where the corpus does not contain authority for a proposition, that is said plainly rather than papered over.

## I. Executive summary

A federal official who (1) operates a social-media account as an instrument of office, (2) opens its comment or reply function to the public for discussion of official business, and (3) blocks a resident from that account because of the resident's expressed views, commits a violation of the First Amendment that is actionable in federal court and, where the other elements are met, a federal crime.

The constitutional violation is not novel. The Second Circuit held in *Knight First Amendment Institute v. Trump* that the interactive space of an official account opened to the public for governance is a public forum, and that blocking a critic from it is viewpoint discrimination: "The Account was intentionally opened for public discussion when the President, upon assuming office, repeatedly used the Account as an official vehicle for governance and made its interactive features accessible to the public without limitation. We hold that this conduct created a public forum." *Knight First Amendment Inst. v. Trump*, 928 F.3d 226, 236 (2d Cir. 2019). The court continued: "If the Account is a forum—public or otherwise—viewpoint discrimination is not permitted." *Id.* The Supreme Court later vacated that judgment as moot after the change of administration, *Biden v. Knight First Amendment Inst. at Columbia Univ.*, 593 U.S. ___ (2021) (mem.), so *Knight* binds no one; but its reasoning is the settled understanding of every court that has addressed the question since, and in *Lindke v. Freed*, 601 U.S. 187 (2024), the Court supplied the state-action test that *Knight* had assumed.

What is novel is the criminal theory, and it is novel only in application, not in principle. 18 U.S.C. § 242 makes it a crime to "willfully subject[] any person ... to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured or protected by the Constitution or laws of the United States" while acting "under color of any law." 18 U.S.C. § 241 makes it a crime for two or more persons to "conspire to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate any person ... in the free exercise or enjoyment of any right or privilege secured to him by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or because of his having so exercised the same." A coordinated practice of viewpoint-based blocking by an official and the staff who administer the account is, on its face, a conspiracy to oppress residents in the exercise of the First Amendment right to speak and to petition, and to do so because they exercised it. 18 U.S.C. § 595 supplies an election-specific count where the purpose is to affect an election. 18 U.S.C. § 2071 supplies a records count where the official conceals or destroys the resulting public record.

Three conclusions follow, and the remainder of this memorandum defends them.

**First, the conduct is government action.** *Lindke* requires the plaintiff to show that the official "(1) had actual authority to speak on behalf of the State on a particular matter, and (2) purported to exercise that authority in the relevant posts." 601 U.S. at 203. For a federal official using an account that carries the office's name, seal, staff, and press apparatus, both elements are ordinarily satisfied — and, critically, the *federal* government has no state-action gap at all: the First Amendment addresses Congress in terms, and a federal officer is the government. *Lindke*'s test identifies when an official is acting officially; it does not create a zone in which a federal officer may use federal authority to censor and call it private.

**Second, the exclusion is viewpoint discrimination, not moderation.** Every forum — traditional, designated, limited, or nonpublic — permits content-neutral rules of decorum and topic. None permits excluding a speaker because of the viewpoint the speaker holds. *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 235–36; *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Blocking is not a rule applied to a comment; it is a status imposed on a person, calibrated to that person's criticism, and it follows the person across every future post. It is the most durable form of viewpoint discrimination available on the platform.

**Third, the criminal statutes fit.** Section 242 requires willfulness, and willfulness is the honest battleground. But willfulness is a fact about the defendant's purpose, not about the clarity of the law; and after *Knight* (2019), the widespread adoption of its rule in the circuits, and *Lindke* (2024), no official can claim that the constitutional line was unknown. The specific intent to silence a particular critic is provable the way intent always is proved: by the timing of the block relative to the criticism, by the selection of targets, by statements about the targets, by the persistence of the practice after notice, and by the absence of any content-neutral criterion that explains the pattern.

The memorandum also states what cannot be done. *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), forecloses a *Bivens* damages action for First Amendment retaliation against a federal officer, and that is a serious loss: it means the ordinary civil remedy is prospective and declaratory relief, statutory civil-rights claims where a conspiracy or a protected class is present, and the administrative and records regimes — not a damages judgment. A prosecutor should say so at the outset, because a memorandum that promised damages would be wrong, and a wrong memorandum is worse than a narrow one.

## II. The conduct defined

Precision here is not pedantry. Every legal conclusion in this memorandum depends on which of the following the defendant did, to whom, on what account, and why. Vagueness in the definition of the conduct would make the criminal theory vulnerable to a due-process challenge and the civil theory vulnerable to a motion to dismiss.

### A. Covered accounts

A **covered account** is a social-media account that a federal official operates, directly or through staff, and that satisfies the *Lindke* inquiry. The following objective indicia are relevant, each of which is drawn from the facts the Supreme Court treated as significant in *Lindke* and from the record in *Knight*. No single indicium is necessary; the question is the character of the account as a whole.

| Indicum | Why it matters | Source |
|---|---|---|
| The account name, handle, or biography identifies the official's title or office | The official "purports to exercise" official authority | *Lindke*, 601 U.S. at 203 |
| The account is linked from, or linked to, an official agency website or press page | The government itself treats the account as its channel | *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 231–33 |
| Government staff draft, post, or moderate content | "an official who uses government staff to make a post will be hard pressed to deny that he was conducting government business" | *Lindke*, 601 U.S. at 202 |
| The account announces policy, personnel, enforcement, or agency action | It is an "official vehicle for governance" | *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 236 |
| The account is used for constituent service, casework, or intake | It functions as an office window | *Lindke* (soliciting feedback from the public) |
| The account's content is scheduled as a federal record | Records law treats it as government business | 44 U.S.C. § 3301(a)(1) |
| The official has described the account as personal, and posts predominantly private material, with no official trappings | Cuts against coverage | *Lindke* (Freed's page was mixed) |

**[INFERENCE]** Where an account is branded with the office and administered by official staff, the *Lindke* showing will ordinarily be satisfiable on the pleadings. Where an account is genuinely mixed — the *Lindke* fact pattern — the analysis must proceed post by post, and blocking, which operates page-wide, is the platform feature that converts a mixed account into a liability trap. The Court said so directly: "The bluntness of Facebook's blocking tool highlights the cost of a 'mixed use' social-media account: If page-wide blocking is the only option, a public official might be unable to prevent someone from commenting on his personal posts without risking liability for also preventing comments on his official posts." *Lindke*, 601 U.S. at 204. **[OBSERVED]**

### B. Covered exclusion

**Blocking** is the platform-level act that prevents a specified account from viewing, replying to, reposting, or otherwise interacting with the blocking account's content. Its mechanics differ by platform, and the differences matter both to the injury and to the proof:

| Mechanism | Effect | Constitutional significance |
|---|---|---|
| Block | Target cannot see, reply to, repost, or message; on some platforms cannot view the profile at all | Excludes from the forum and severs the right to receive |
| Mute | Target can see and reply; the official does not see the replies | Does not exclude; weak basis for a claim |
| Restrict / hide reply | Target's replies are visible to the target and to the target's followers but not to the general audience | Partial exclusion; burden rather than ban |
| Shadow restriction | Target's content is de-amplified without notice | Burden; requires technical proof |
| Deleting a comment | Removes the expression from the thread | Destruction of the record and of the speech |

**[INFERENCE]** Blocking is the strongest case because it is total, durable, status-based, and visible. Restriction and shadow measures are weaker but not irrelevant: the First Amendment reaches burdens as well as bans. *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 234 ("burdens to speech as well as outright bans run afoul of the First Amendment"), quoting *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552, 566 (2011). **[OBSERVED]**

### C. The requisite reason

The constitutional and criminal theories both require that the exclusion be **because of** the resident's protected speech or viewpoint. Three evidentiary categories prove that reason:

1. **Direct** — statements by the official or staff about the target ("troll," "bot," "deranged," "enemy"), or instructions to "clean up" the replies after critical coverage.
2. **Circumstantial** — the block follows a specific criticism within a short interval; the blocked set is ideologically or topically one-sided; the official's own posts invite the very criticism that triggered the block.
3. **Structural** — the account's written moderation policy, if any, contains no neutral criterion capable of explaining the pattern, or the official concedes that no policy exists.

**[INFERENCE]** The third category is where most cases will be won or lost. A government actor who can point to a published, content-neutral rule — no threats, no doxxing, no spam, no commercial solicitation — and who applied it evenhandedly, has a defense. A government actor who blocked critics and let identically-worded supporters through has conceded the element.

### D. What is not covered

This memorandum does not argue that officials must tolerate threats, harassment, doxxing, obscenity, or spam. Every forum permits those exclusions; *Knight* itself recognized that the government may enforce reasonable, viewpoint-neutral rules. Nor does it argue that an official must maintain a social-media account at all. The claim is narrower and stronger: **once the government opens a forum for public comment on official business, it may not expel a person from that forum because of what the person said.**

## III. Government action: why the account is the government's

### A. The federal government has no state-action gap

Section 1983 is the ordinary vehicle for constitutional torts, and it is expressly limited to persons acting "under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, of any State or Territory or the District of Columbia." 42 U.S.C. § 1983. That limitation is deliberate, and it is why a resident cannot sue a federal officer under § 1983 for acting under federal law. The First Amendment, by contrast, binds the federal government directly — its operative words are 'Congress shall make no law' — and reaches the States through the Fourteenth. **[INFERENCE]** The consequence is structural and it favors the government-obligation argument: a federal official cannot escape the First Amendment by pointing to the absence of state action, because the question for a federal defendant is not *whether* the government acted but *whether the official acted as the government*.

### B. *Lindke*'s test, applied to a federal officer

*Lindke v. Freed* supplies that test. The Court held that "a public official's social-media activity constitutes state action under §1983 only if the official (1) possessed actual authority to speak on the State's behalf, and (2) purported to exercise that authority." 601 U.S. 187, 198 (2024). **[OBSERVED]** The Court restated the test at the close of its opinion in the form the plaintiff must satisfy: "(1) had actual authority to speak on behalf of the State on a particular matter, and (2) purported to exercise that authority in the relevant posts." *Id.* at 203. **[OBSERVED]**

One footnote in *Lindke* does more work than most opinions. "An official cannot insulate government business from scrutiny by conducting it on a personal page." *Id.* at 202 n.2. **[OBSERVED]** That sentence is the answer to the defense that has defeated most blocking claims, and it is the sentence a prosecutor should put in the indictment's introduction.

The first element is a question of authority: "Determining the scope of an official's power requires careful attention to the relevant statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage." *Id.* at 199. For federal officers, the sources of authority are the organic statute, the delegation of authority to the agency, the agency's own regulations and orders, and the settled practice of the office. **[INFERENCE]** A Cabinet secretary's authority to "speak for the Department" is conferred by the Secretary's statutory charge and by the Department's communications orders; a White House press secretary's authority is conferred by the President's appointment and delegation; an agency's regional office's authority is conferred by the agency's own delegation instruments. Each is discoverable, and each should be proved rather than assumed.

The second element is a question of conduct: whether the official "purported to exercise that authority in the relevant posts." *Id.* at 203. The Court's examples are instructive. A city manager who "used government staff to make a post will be hard pressed to deny that he was conducting government business." *Id.* at 202. **[OBSERVED]** And the Court emphasized that the analysis is post-specific: "it is crucial for the plaintiff to show that the official is purporting to exercise state authority in specific posts." *Id.*

**[INFERENCE]** Two consequences follow for federal accounts. First, the analysis must be done post by post, which is exactly why blocking is so dangerous: a block is page-wide, and the official who blocks a critic from *all* posts has necessarily blocked that critic from the posts that were official business. Second, the government's own communications architecture is evidence: if the account is listed on the agency's website, if the agency's press releases link to it, if agency staff operate it, the official "purported to exercise" authority in it as a matter of course.

### C. The *Knight* record, and why it will be easy to replicate

The *Knight* court's state-action analysis rested on facts that are documented, not subjective: the account was used "as an official vehicle for governance," its interactive features were "accessible to the public without limitation," and the government itself conceded that the account was not "independent of [the President's] presidency." 928 F.3d at 230, 236. **[OBSERVED]** Those facts are provable with public records: the account's biography, the agency's website, archived posts, press releases, and staff testimony.

The court also rejected the argument that the account was private because the official happened to use it personally: "a public official and his subordinates hold out and use a social media account open to the public as an official account for conducting official business. That account has interactive features open to the public, making public interaction a prominent feature of the account. These factors mean that the account is not private." *Id.* at 235. **[OBSERVED]** And it held that the exclusion was from "government-controlled property": "the President excluded the Individual Plaintiffs from government-controlled property when he used the blocking function of the Account to exclude disfavored voices." *Id.* **[OBSERVED]**

### D. The "personal account" defense

The strongest defense to a blocking claim is that the account is personal, and *Lindke* arms it. An official who "keeps personal posts in a clearly designated personal account" gets "a heavy (though not irrebuttable) presumption that all of the posts on his page were personal." 601 U.S. at 202. **[OBSERVED]** And the Court stated the stakes plainly: "if Freed acted in his private capacity when he blocked Lindke and deleted his comments, he did not violate Lindke's First Amendment rights—instead, he exercised his own." *Id.* at 194. **[OBSERVED]** But the defense has a ceiling. It cannot succeed where the account is the office's channel, and it cannot succeed as to the official posts on a mixed account, because blocking is page-wide. The Court said exactly that: because "blocking operated on a page-wide basis, a court would have to consider whether Freed had engaged in state action with respect to any post on which Lindke wished to comment." *Id.* at 204. **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** For a federal official with a predominantly official account, the defense is not merely weak; it is unavailable.

## IV. The forum: the comment thread is not the government's megaphone

### A. The three categories, and the rule that governs all of them

Government property is a traditional public forum, a designated public forum, a limited public forum, or a nonpublic forum. *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45–46 (1983). The classification determines the standard of review for *access* rules. But one rule cuts across every category: **viewpoint discrimination is impermissible in all of them.** *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 235, citing *Pleasant Grove City v. Summum*, 555 U.S. 460, 469–70 (2009), and *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. **[OBSERVED]** That is why the forum fight, though important, is not dispositive: even if a court were to hold that an official's comment thread is a nonpublic forum, the official still could not exclude a person because of the person's viewpoint.

The Second Circuit stated the principle in its opening paragraph, before any doctrinal machinery: the First Amendment "does not permit a public official who utilizes a social media account for all manner of official purposes to exclude persons from an otherwise-open online dialogue because they expressed views with which the official disagrees." *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 230. **[OBSERVED]** Everything that follows is the working-out of that sentence.

### B. *Knight*'s holding

The Second Circuit held that the account was a designated public forum. Its reasoning is short enough to quote in full:

> "The Account was intentionally opened for public discussion when the President, upon assuming office, repeatedly used the Account as an official vehicle for governance and made its interactive features accessible to the public without limitation. We hold that this conduct created a public forum."

*Knight*, 928 F.3d at 236. **[OBSERVED]**

Two features of that reasoning matter for every later case. First, the forum was created by *use*: the government "intentionally opened" the space by conducting official business in it and leaving the replies open. It did not need a formal designation, a policy, or a sign. Second, the forum is the *interactive space* — the replies, reposts, and likes — not the official's own posts. The court was explicit that the official's own tweets are government speech and that the case "does not turn on the President's initial tweets; it turns on his supervision of the interactive features of the Account." *Id.* at 239. **[OBSERVED]** That distinction is the answer to the government-speech defense, and it is the single most important doctrinal move in this area.

### C. The government-speech defense, and why it fails

The government may say what it wishes. *Pleasant Grove*, 555 U.S. at 467 ("The Free Speech Clause restricts government regulation of private speech; it does not regulate government speech."). It may select the monuments in a park, *id.*, the message on a license plate, *Walker v. Texas Div., Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.*, 576 U.S. 200 (2015), and the flag it raises, *Shurtleff v. City of Boston*, 596 U.S. 243 (2022).

**[INFERENCE]** None of that reaches the present case, for three reasons.

*First*, the speech at issue is not the government's. When a resident replies to a post, "the message is identified as coming from that user, not from the President," and the government "does not argue that the President has attempted to exercise any control over the messages of others, except to the extent he has blocked some persons expressing viewpoints he finds distasteful." *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 239. **[OBSERVED]** The government-speech doctrine governs the government's own message; it does not license the government to silence everyone else's.

The court made the point exhaustive: "while the President's tweets can accurately be described as government speech, the retweets, replies, and likes of other users ... are not government speech under any formulation." *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 239. **[OBSERVED]**

*Second*, the government-speech doctrine concerns *what the government says*, not *who may answer*. An official who posts a policy announcement and then blocks a critic is not editing the government's message. He is excluding a participant. *Knight* put the point beyond doubt: "the government is not permitted to 'amplify' favored speech by banning or burdening viewpoints with which it disagrees." *Id.* at 238. **[OBSERVED]**

*Matal* supplies the limiting principle: "If private speech could be passed off as government speech by simply affixing a government seal of approval, government could silence or muffle the expression of disfavored viewpoints." *Matal v. Tam*, 582 U.S. 218 (2017). **[OBSERVED]** And *Walker* itself asks whether the government "direct[ly] control[s] over the messages conveyed" — a question that a comment thread, populated by strangers, answers no. *Walker v. Texas Div., Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.*, 576 U.S. 200 (2015). **[OBSERVED]**

*Third*, the doctrine cannot be stretched to cover a forum the government created and then selectively closed. *Walker* and *Shurtleff* both turned on the government's control over the message and the absence of a public forum; where the government has opened the space to all comers, the forum analysis governs, and viewpoint neutrality is the price of opening it.

### D. Even a nonpublic forum does not save the official

If a court were to classify the thread as a nonpublic forum, the government could impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions on access. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806. But "[v]iewpoint discrimination is ... an egregious form of content discrimination," *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995), and it is barred even in a nonpublic forum. *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 235 (citing *Cornelius*). **[OBSERVED]** The official who blocks critics while permitting supporters has not applied a reasonable restriction; he has applied a viewpoint test.

**[INFERENCE]** The nonpublic-forum holding, if it comes, therefore narrows the remedy rather than eliminating the violation: it would defeat a claim of right to *post* in the thread, but not a claim that the *exclusion* was unconstitutional. That is the correct way to plead in the alternative, and it is how the civil complaint in this matter should be structured.

### E. Blocking is not moderation

Moderation is a rule applied to content: this comment is spam, that one is a threat, this one is off-topic. Blocking is a status applied to a person: you may not speak here again, on any topic, for any reason, ever. The difference is not semantic. A content rule can be tested for neutrality by comparing similarly situated comments; a block cannot, because it operates on identity rather than content.

**[INFERENCE]** That asymmetry is why blocking is so probative of viewpoint: the official had to *select a person*, and the selection is the evidence. It is also why the practice is so vulnerable to a criminal charge, because selection supplies the specific intent that § 242 requires.

## V. The discrimination: viewpoint, retaliation, and the rights of the audience

### A. Viewpoint discrimination

The rule is settled: "Viewpoint discrimination is thus an egregious form of content discrimination. The government must abstain from regulating speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction." *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). **[REPORTED]** In *Iancu v. Brunetti*, 588 U.S. 388 (2019), the Court put it in five words: "Viewpoint discrimination is poison to a free society." **[INFERENCE]** The blocking official's conduct falls within the core of the prohibition: the resident is excluded not for how she said it, or where, or when, but for the position she took.

### B. Retaliation

Blocking in response to criticism is also actionable as retaliation. The elements are that the plaintiff engaged in protected speech, that the defendant took adverse action, and that the speech was a but-for cause of the action. *Nieves v. Bartlett*, 587 U.S. 391 (2019), adds a probable-cause requirement in the arrest context, which has no analogue here: there is no warrant, no charge, and no probable cause to consider. *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250 (2006), confirms that retaliatory motive is a proper subject of a constitutional claim. **[INFERENCE]** In a blocking case the causation question is unusually clean: the official need not have arrested anyone, and the only plausible explanation for a durable, person-specific exclusion is the person's speech.

### C. The right to receive

Blocking injures the resident twice: as speaker, and as listener. The Supreme Court has long protected the right to receive information and ideas. *Lamont v. Postmaster General*, 381 U.S. 301 (1965); *Virginia State Bd. of Pharmacy v. Virginia Citizens Consumer Council*, 425 U.S. 748, 756 (1976). **[OBSERVED]** *Lindke* itself documents the severity of the listener injury: "On some platforms, a blocked user might be unable even to see the blocker's posts," citing the Ninth Circuit's finding that "on Twitter, once a user has been 'blocked,' the individual can neither interact with nor view the blocker's Twitter feed." 601 U.S. at 204 n.3. **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** The right-to-receive theory has two practical advantages. It gives standing to residents who never commented but who are blocked from reading, and it defeats the "you can log out and look" defense, because the burden on access is itself the injury. *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 234 (workarounds do not cure the violation). **[OBSERVED]**

### D. The right to petition

The First Amendment protects "the right of the people ... to petition the Government for a redress of grievances," and the right is not confined to the legislature: "Certainly the right to petition extends to all departments of the Government." *California Motor Transport Co. v. Trucking Unlimited*, 404 U.S. 508, 510 (1972). **[REPORTED]** A constituent's reply to an official's post about official business is a petition in the ordinary sense: it is addressed to the officer, about the officer's conduct of office, in the channel the officer created for that purpose. **[INFERENCE]** Blocking a constituent from that channel burdens the petition right independently of the speech right, and it does so in the one place the government has invited the petition.

### E. Burdens, not just bans

The First Amendment reaches "burdens to speech as well as outright bans." *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 234. A restriction that leaves the resident able to speak elsewhere but unable to speak *here*, to *this* audience, about *this* official's conduct, is a burden on the very speech the forum was created to host. *Id.* at 234–35. **[OBSERVED]**

## VI. Why the violation is not cured by alternatives

Three defenses are commonly offered, and each fails on the authority.

**"The resident can still read the posts by logging out."** The Second Circuit rejected this: the government conceded that "these 'workarounds' burden the Individual Plaintiffs' speech," and "the fact that the Individual Plaintiffs retain some ability to 'work around' the blocking does not cure the constitutional violation." *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 234. **[OBSERVED]**

**"The resident can post elsewhere."** Rejected for the same reason: "Neither does the fact that the Individual Plaintiffs can post messages elsewhere on Twitter." *Id.* **[OBSERVED]**

**"The official has a right not to listen."** True but irrelevant. "A person's right to speak is not infringed when government simply ignores that person while listening to others." *Minnesota State Bd. for Cmty. Colls. v. Knight*, 465 U.S. 271, 288 (1984), quoted in *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 237. **[OBSERVED]** But the blocked residents "were not simply ignored by the President, their ability to speak to the rest of the public users of the Account was burdened." *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 237. **[OBSERVED]** Ignoring is permitted; silencing is not.

**Part III — Civil liability**

## VII. The civil case: what can be recovered, from whom, and in what capacity

A prosecutor's memorandum that ignored the civil side would be incomplete, and a civil-rights memorandum that promised damages against a federal officer would be false. This Part states the remedies that exist, the ones that do not, and the pleading structure that preserves both.

### A. Section 1983 and the federal-officer gap

Section 1983 creates liability for "[e]very person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, of any State or Territory or the District of Columbia," deprives another of constitutional rights. 42 U.S.C. § 1983. **[OBSERVED]** The statute's list is exhaustive of the sovereigns it reaches, and the Supreme Court has held that federal officers acting under federal law are not within it. **[INFERENCE]** The textual point is decisive on its face: § 1983 names the States, the Territories, and the District of Columbia; it does not name the United States.

The Supreme Court said so directly: "no general statute making federal officers liable for acts committed 'under color,' but in violation, of their federal authority has been passed." *Wheeldin v. Wheeler*, 373 U.S. 647, 651–52 (1963). **[REPORTED]** The circuits apply it without qualification: "Section 1983 ... is inapplicable to persons acting under color of federal law." *Yassin v. Weyker*, 39 F.4th 1086 (8th Cir. 2022). **[REPORTED]**

That gap has three consequences, and each must be pleaded around rather than ignored.

**First**, a claim against a federal officer for acts under federal law must be brought as a direct constitutional claim, not under § 1983. **Second**, § 1983 remains fully available against state and local officials who engage in the same conduct — which matters because federal programs are frequently administered through state and local partners, and because a federal officer who directs or jointly participates in a state actor's blocking may be reached as a joint actor. **Third**, a federal officer who conspires with state or local officials to block residents can be sued under § 1983 as a co-conspirator even though he is a federal officer, because the state actor supplies the element the statute requires.

**[INFERENCE]** In practice, the joint-actor route is worth pleading in every case where a federal official's account is administered with a state agency, a task force, or a contractor that is itself a state actor. The facts that establish joint action are the same facts that establish the conspiracy count under 18 U.S.C. § 241, so the civil and criminal theories share an evidentiary core.

### B. *Bivens* and *Egbert*: the damages door is closed

For half a century, *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), supplied a damages remedy against federal officers for constitutional violations. **[OBSERVED]** In *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), the Court closed that door for the claim at issue here. The holding is stated without qualification: "There is no *Bivens* cause of action for Boule's First Amendment retaliation claim." 596 U.S. at 502. **[OBSERVED]** And the Court's reason is directly on point for a blocking case:

> "extending *Bivens* to alleged First Amendment violations would pose an acute 'risk that fear of personal monetary liability and harassing litigation will unduly inhibit officials in the discharge of their duties.'"

*Id.*, quoting *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 638 (1987). **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** Two conclusions follow, and they should be stated in the complaint's opening paragraph rather than discovered at the motion to dismiss. First, a damages claim against a federal officer for viewpoint-based blocking is, after *Egbert*, very likely foreclosed. Second, *Egbert* does not touch prospective relief. The Court's analysis is about "a damages action," "monetary liability," and "the discharge of duties"; it says nothing about an injunction ordering an official to stop violating the Constitution. That distinction is the spine of the civil case.

### C. Prospective relief: the injunction that actually works

A suit against a federal officer for prospective, non-monetary relief is not a suit against the United States. **5 U.S.C. § 702** waives sovereign immunity for exactly that: "An action in a court of the United States seeking relief other than money damages and stating a claim that an agency or an officer or employee thereof acted or failed to act in an official capacity or under color of legal authority shall not be dismissed nor relief therein be denied on the ground that it is against the United States." **[OBSERVED]** The waiver requires that "any mandatory or injunctive decree shall specify the Federal officer or officers (by name or by title), and their successors in office, personally responsible for compliance." **[OBSERVED]** That is a pleading instruction, and it should be followed literally.

Three remedial statutes supply the rest of the architecture:

| Statute | Function | Text |
|---|---|---|
| 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A), (B) | Sets aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, or "contrary to constitutional right, power, privilege, or immunity" | Quoted above |
| 5 U.S.C. § 706(1) | Compels "agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed" | Quoted above |
| 28 U.S.C. § 1361 | Mandamus to "compel an officer or employee of the United States ... to perform a duty owed to the plaintiff" | Quoted above |
| 28 U.S.C. § 2201 | Declaratory judgment in a "case of actual controversy" | Quoted above |

The equitable route does not depend on the APA at all. *Ex parte Young* holds that an officer enforcing an unconstitutional practice "is in that case stripped of his official or representative character and is subjected in his person to the consequences of his individual conduct." 209 U.S. 123, 159–60 (1908). **[REPORTED]** *Larson v. Domestic & Foreign Commerce Corp.* adds that "where the officer's powers are limited by statute, his actions beyond those limitations are considered individual and not sovereign actions." 337 U.S. 682, 689 (1949). **[REPORTED]** **[INFERENCE]** Because no statute authorizes an official to exclude a resident on viewpoint grounds, the conduct is ultra vires in the precise sense *Larson* describes, and the injunction runs against the person, not the Treasury.

The APA is the weaker of the two routes, and the memorandum should say so. Section 704 limits review to "final agency action for which there is no other adequate remedy in a court," and *Bennett v. Spear* requires that the action "mark the 'consummation' of the agency's decisionmaking process" and be one "by which 'rights or obligations have been determined,' or from which 'legal consequences will flow.'" 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997). **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** A single block by a single staffer is operational moderation, not the consummation of anything, and it is a poor candidate for review. A *published policy* of blocking critics is a different animal: it is a rule of general applicability, it determines who may speak, and it is reviewable. The APA count should therefore be pleaded against the policy, with the equitable count pleaded against the officer.

Mandamus under § 1361 is a backstop. It requires a clear, ministerial duty and no adequate alternative remedy, and courts grant it sparingly. **[INFERENCE]** It is best pleaded as an alternative count aimed at the *duty to comply with the First Amendment* in administering a government forum, not at any discretionary judgment about content.

### D. Section 1985(3): the conspiracy claim that reaches federal officers

Section 1985(3) is the one civil-rights statute that reaches a conspiracy without requiring that any conspirator be a state actor. It provides a damages action where "two or more persons ... conspire ... for the purpose of depriving, either directly or indirectly, any person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws, or of equal privileges and immunities under the laws," and "one or more persons engaged therein do ... any act in furtherance of the object of such conspiracy, whereby another is injured in his person or property, or deprived of having and exercising any right or privilege of a citizen of the United States." 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3). **[OBSERVED]**

Two limits must be confronted. **First**, the Supreme Court requires "some racial, or perhaps otherwise class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus." *Griffin v. Breckenridge*, 403 U.S. 88, 102 (1971). **Second**, the Court has refused to treat ideological groups as classes for this purpose. *Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic*, 506 U.S. 263 (1993); *United Bhd. of Carpenters & Joiners v. Scott*, 463 U.S. 825 (1983). **[INFERENCE]** The circuits have drawn the conclusion: "we hold that § 1985(3) does not provide a cause of action for individuals allegedly injured by conspiracies motivated by discriminatory animus directed toward their political affiliation." *Farber v. City of Paterson*, 440 F.3d 131, 142–43 (3d Cir. 2006). **[REPORTED]** Those cases are a serious obstacle to a § 1985(3) claim premised solely on viewpoint. The claim survives where the exclusion also targets a protected class — race, religion, national origin, sex, or disability — or where the conspiracy's object is to prevent a class of residents from exercising a right of citizenship, which is the statute's own language. A complaint should plead § 1985(3) in the alternative and should not depend on it.

Section 1986 adds a claim against a supervisor who "has knowledge that any of the wrongs conspired to be done" are about to be committed and who "neglects or refuses" to prevent them, but "no action under the provisions of this section shall be sustained which is not commenced within one year" of the wrong. 42 U.S.C. § 1986. **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** The one-year limit makes § 1986 a trap for the diligent-but-slow plaintiff; it should be pleaded only when the facts and the calendar fit. **[INFERENCE]** In an agency where communications staff block critics at scale, § 1986 reaches the official who knew and did nothing — the precise defendant a damages suit would otherwise miss.

### E. Statutory civil regimes that apply without a constitutional tort

Three statutory schemes supply civil consequences independent of *Bivens*.

**The Hatch Act — narrowly.** 5 U.S.C. § 7323(a)(1) provides that an employee "may not ... use his official authority or influence for the purpose of interfering with or affecting the result of an election." **[OBSERVED]** Enforcement is by the Office of Special Counsel, with discipline up to removal. 5 U.S.C. § 7326. **[INFERENCE]** The fit is narrower than it first appears. "Political activity" is defined as "an activity directed toward the success or failure of a political party, candidate for partisan political office, or partisan political group," 5 C.F.R. § 734.101, and § 7322(1) excludes the President and Vice President from the Act's coverage entirely. **[OBSERVED]** Blocking a critic of agency performance, without an electoral object, is not political activity. Where the object *is* electoral — a campaign-season purge of the official's opponents — the OSC complaint is a realistic remedy that needs neither *Bivens* nor damages. **[INFERENCE]** Charge it only on that fact pattern.

**Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act.** 29 U.S.C. § 794 bars disability discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance. **[INFERENCE]** Where a resident's only practical access to an agency's announcements is the account, and the resident is excluded because of a disability-related communication (for example, a blind resident whose screen-reader workflow depends on the thread), § 504 supplies a claim that does not require viewpoint analysis.

**Title VI.** 42 U.S.C. § 2000d bars race, color, and national-origin discrimination in federally assisted programs. **[INFERENCE]** It applies where the blocking pattern correlates with protected status — a claim that should be pleaded on the facts, not assumed.

### E-bis. Unconstitutional conditions

There is a second, independent way to frame the civil wrong that does not depend on forum classification. Even where a person has "no 'right' to a valuable governmental benefit," the government "may not deny a benefit to a person on a basis that infringes his constitutionally protected interests—especially, his interest in freedom of speech." *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972). **[OBSERVED]** And "[t]o deny an exemption to claimants who engage in certain forms of speech is in effect to penalize them for such speech." *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958). **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** The doctrine supplies a label for what blocking is — the withdrawal of a government-conferred benefit (the ability to participate in the official's channel) because of protected speech — and it supplies a remedy rationale: an injunction restoring the benefit, not damages. It does not itself create a cause of action, which is why it belongs in the complaint as a theory of violation rather than as a separate count.

### F. Records law: the civil and criminal overlap

An official account used for official business generates federal records. **44 U.S.C. § 3301(a)(1)(A)** defines "records" to include "all recorded information, regardless of form or characteristics, made or received by a Federal agency under Federal law or in connection with the transaction of public business," and § 3301(a)(2) makes clear that "recorded information" includes information "created, manipulated, communicated, or stored in digital or electronic form." **[OBSERVED]** **44 U.S.C. § 3101** requires each agency head to "make and preserve records containing adequate and proper documentation of the organization, functions, policies, decisions, procedures, and essential transactions of the agency." **[OBSERVED]** **44 U.S.C. § 3106** requires the agency head to notify the Archivist of "any actual, impending, or threatened unlawful removal, defacing, alteration, corruption, deletion, erasure, or other destruction of records in the custody of the agency," and to "initiate action through the Attorney General for the recovery of records." **[OBSERVED]** Where the agency head does not act, "the Archivist shall request the Attorney General to initiate such an action, and shall notify the Congress." **[OBSERVED]**

**44 U.S.C. § 3314** makes the point that turns a deletion into a violation: "The procedures prescribed by this chapter are exclusive, and records of the United States Government may not be alienated or destroyed except under this chapter." **[OBSERVED]** Deletion outside a NARA-approved schedule is therefore unlawful destruction, not administrative housekeeping.

The regulations close the last gap — whether a citizen's comment is "received" by the agency at all. **36 C.F.R. § 1222.10(b)(4)** defines "[r]eceived" as "the acceptance or collection of documentary materials by or on behalf of an agency or agency personnel in the course of their official duties regardless of their origin," "regardless of how transmitted (in person or by messenger, mail, electronic means, or by any other method)." **[OBSERVED]** And **36 C.F.R. § 1222.20(b)(2)** provides that "[i]f information about private matters and agency business appears in a received document, the document is a Federal record." **[OBSERVED]** A public reply about agency business, transmitted electronically to an official account, is a federal record on the face of those provisions.

**[INFERENCE]** That last sentence of § 3106(b) is the most underused lever in this area. A resident who documents the deletion of comments from an official account can force a referral to the Attorney General and a notification to Congress without filing a lawsuit at all — and the referral itself creates a record that supports the criminal counts in Part IV.

The private-right-of-action question is separate and must be answered honestly. The Supreme Court has said there is none: "No provision of either Act, however, expressly confers a right of action on private parties. Nor do we believe that such a private right of action can be implied." *Kissinger v. Reporters Comm. for Freedom of the Press*, 445 U.S. 136, 148–49 (1980). **[REPORTED]** The D.C. Circuit has preserved exactly one narrow route: private litigants "may bring suit to require the agency head and Archivist to fulfill their statutory duty to notify Congress and ask the Attorney General to initiate legal action," even though the FRA "precludes direct private actions to require that agency staff comply with the agency's recordkeeping guidelines." *Armstrong v. Bush*, 924 F.2d 282 (D.C. Cir. 1991). **[REPORTED]** **[INFERENCE]** The remedy is therefore the § 3106 chain, a FOIA request, and the criminal statute — not a civil damages claim, and not an order restoring the deleted comments.

One further limit belongs here rather than in a footnote: **a block is not a deletion.** A block hides the blocker's view of the target; the target's comments ordinarily remain in the thread for everyone else. **[INFERENCE]** Blocking alone therefore destroys no record and supports no records count. The records theory attaches only where the official deletes or hides replies from the public record — which is a separate act that must be proved separately.

### G. Qualified immunity, and why it does not end the case

Qualified immunity protects officials from damages liability unless the violated right was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. **[INFERENCE]** Three points follow. **First**, qualified immunity does not bar prospective relief; it is a defense to damages. **Second**, for damages, the right at issue here has been clearly established since at least 2019 in the Second and Fourth Circuits, and since 2024 nationwide by *Lindke*. *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 236; *Davison v. Randall*, 912 F.3d 666 (4th Cir. 2019); *Lindke*, 601 U.S. at 203. **Third**, *Egbert* already forecloses the damages claim against federal officers, so qualified immunity is largely academic on the federal side — the doctrine matters most for state and local co-defendants. And there is adverse authority on the state side that must be confronted rather than ignored: *Dingwell v. Cossette* (D. Conn. 2020) granted qualified immunity to a police chief for blocking, holding that "Plaintiff's First Amendment right to comment on the MPD Facebook page was not clearly established in 2015." **[OBSERVED]** The answer is timing: *Knight* was decided in 2019. Conduct before 2019 may be immunized; conduct after it may not.

## VIII. 18 U.S.C. § 242: deprivation of rights under color of law

### A. The statute

> "Whoever, under color of any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, or custom, willfully subjects any person in any State, Territory, Commonwealth, Possession, or District to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured or protected by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or to different punishments, pains, or penalties, on account of such person being an alien, or by reason of his color, or race, than are prescribed for the punishment of citizens, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than one year, or both; and if bodily injury results from the acts committed in violation of this section or if such acts include the use, attempted use, or threatened use of a dangerous weapon, explosives, or fire, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than ten years, or both; and if death results from the acts committed in violation of this section or if such acts include kidnapping or an attempt to kidnap, aggravated sexual abuse, or an attempt to commit aggravated sexual abuse, or an attempt to kill, shall be fined under this title, or imprisoned for any term of years or for life, or both, or may be sentenced to death."

18 U.S.C. § 242. **[OBSERVED]**

### B. Elements

**[INFERENCE]** A charge under § 242 requires proof of four things:

1. **The defendant acted under color of law.** The defendant must have acted in the performance of, or under pretense of, an official duty, using power conferred by law. The statute's phrase is "under color of any law, statute, ordinance, regulation, or custom."
2. **The defendant acted willfully.** The defendant must have intended to deprive the victim of a right secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States — not merely intended the physical act.
3. **The victim was deprived of such a right.** The right must be one the Constitution or a federal statute secures.
4. **The victim was a person "in any State, Territory, Commonwealth, Possession, or District."**

The penalty is a misdemeanor term of up to one year for the base offense, with escalating felonies where bodily injury, a dangerous weapon, kidnapping, or death is involved. **[OBSERVED]** A blocking prosecution will ordinarily be a base-offense misdemeanor, which is itself a strategic fact: it is triable to a district judge, it needs no death-penalty protocol, and its one-year exposure makes it a realistic charging decision rather than a rhetorical one.

### C. "Under color of any law" reaches federal officers

This is the element on which the entire federal theory depends, and it is worth stating carefully because most of the case law under § 242 involves state and local officers.

The text answers the question. Section 242 says "under color of **any** law" — not "any State law." Congress knew how to limit a civil-rights statute to state action when it wanted to: § 1983, enacted in the same Reconstruction period, reaches only persons acting under color of "any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, **of any State or Territory or the District of Columbia**." 42 U.S.C. § 1983. **[OBSERVED]** The contrast is deliberate and it is dispositive on the face of the two statutes: where Congress enumerated the sovereigns, it meant to limit them; where it wrote "any law," it meant any law.

The Supreme Court read the statute that way in the case that saved it from vagueness. "He who acts under color of law may be a federal officer or a state officer. He may act under color of federal law or of state law." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 108 (1945). **[OBSERVED]** And the Ninth Circuit, confronted with the argument that § 242 covers only action under color of state law, rejected it as finding no support in the statutory text, and affirmed the § 242 convictions of two Border Patrol agents. *United States v. Otherson*, 637 F.2d 1276, 1278–79 (9th Cir. 1981). **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** The structure of § 242 confirms the reading. The statute's object is the abuse of official power, and its concern is the *misuse of law*, not the identity of the sovereign that conferred it. A federal officer who uses federal authority to deprive a resident of a federal constitutional right is the paradigm case the statute describes, not an exception to it. This reading is also the only one consistent with the statute's purpose: a rule that immunized federal officers from the one criminal civil-rights statute aimed at official abuse would invert the statute's design.

**[INFERENCE]** Two practical consequences follow. First, the indictment should plead the source of the defendant's authority — the statute, regulation, delegation order, or settled custom — because that is what "under color of law" means in practice. Second, the defense will argue that § 242 is a state-action statute; the government's answer is the text, the contrast with § 1983, and the absurdity of reading the statute to protect against local tyranny while licensing federal tyranny.

> **Verification note.** The bound reporters for *Screws* (1945), *Lanier* (1997), and *Ehrlichman* (D.C. Cir. 1976) are not stored as text in the retrieved corpus, which thins out before the 1990s. The *Screws* passage quoted in this Part was verified through a later opinion that reproduces it verbatim. The *Lanier* and *Ehrlichman* propositions are stated from their holdings and must be cite-checked against the bound volumes before any filing; *Ehrlichman*'s significance is that a White House official was convicted under § 241, which is a fact about the docket as much as about the opinion.

### D. Willfulness

**[INFERENCE]** "Willfully" in § 242 is not satisfied by negligence, and it is not satisfied by the mere intent to press a button. The Supreme Court read the word to require a specific intent to deprive the victim of a federal right, and it read the statute narrowly for that reason: a statute that criminalized the unintentional deprivation of vaguely defined rights would be unconstitutional for vagueness. *Screws*, 325 U.S. 91. The Court later clarified that the right must have been made specific by statute or case law, so that the defendant had fair warning of the right he was accused of violating, but that the government need not prove the defendant knew his conduct was unconstitutional or that a prior case had matched his facts. *Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259.

That standard is met here, and the proof is ordinary circumstantial proof of intent:

| Fact | What it proves |
|---|---|
| The official blocked the resident within hours of a specific criticism | Temporal proximity to protected speech |
| The official left identically-worded supportive comments in place | Selection on viewpoint, not content |
| The official or staff described the resident as a "troll," "bot," or "enemy" | Animus toward the speaker |
| The block persisted after the official was told, by counsel or by public reporting, that blocking critics violates the First Amendment | Deliberate persistence after notice |
| The official's own account invited comment on the subject matter | Knowledge that the space was a forum |
| No published, content-neutral rule explains the selection | Absence of a neutral explanation |

**[INFERENCE]** The last row is the one that converts a civil case into a criminal one. A public official who has been advised, in writing, that a practice is unconstitutional, and who continues the practice while targeting critics, has done more than act with knowledge; he has acted with the purpose § 242 requires.

### E. The right deprived

The right is the First Amendment right to speak in a government forum without viewpoint discrimination, together with the rights to receive information and to petition. Those rights are "secured by the Constitution" in the plainest sense, and they have been made specific by *Knight*, *Davison*, and *Lindke*. **[INFERENCE]** The indictment should name the right precisely — "the right to be free from viewpoint discrimination in a designated public forum" — rather than gesturing at "free speech," because specificity is both a pleading requirement and the answer to a vagueness defense.

## IX. 18 U.S.C. § 241: conspiracy against rights

### A. The statute

> "If two or more persons conspire to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate any person in any State, Territory, Commonwealth, Possession, or District in the free exercise or enjoyment of any right or privilege secured to him by the Constitution or laws of the United States, or because of his having so exercised the same; or If two or more persons go in disguise on the highway, or on the premises of another, with intent to prevent or hinder his free exercise or enjoyment of any right or privilege so secured— They shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than ten years, or both..."

18 U.S.C. § 241. **[OBSERVED]**

### B. Why § 241 is the better charge

**[INFERENCE]** Section 241 is, for three reasons, the more natural charge in a coordinated-blocking case.

**First, it names the right act.** The statute reaches conspiracies to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate a person "in the free exercise or enjoyment of any right," **or because of his having so exercised the same**. That second clause is a retaliation provision written into the statute in 1870. A resident who is blocked *because* she criticized the official has been injured "because of his having so exercised" the right of speech. The fit is exact, and it does not require the government to characterize blocking as anything other than what it is.

**Second, it requires no state action.** Section 241 reaches private conspirators as well as officials. That means the communications director, the press aide, the digital strategist, and the outside consultant who agreed on the block list are all within the statute, not just the official whose name is on the account. **[INFERENCE]** It also means the charge survives even if the principal's "official action" defense succeeds: the conspiracy charge does not depend on the principal's state action, only on the agreement and the object.

**Third, it carries a ten-year maximum.** The base § 242 offense is a one-year misdemeanor. Section 241 is a felony with a ten-year maximum. **[OBSERVED]** A charging decision that ignores § 241 would leave the most serious available penalty on the table.

### C. Elements

**[INFERENCE]** The elements are:

1. **An agreement** between two or more persons to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate a person in the exercise of a federal right, or because the person exercised it.
2. **Specific intent** to achieve the object of the agreement.
3. **An overt act** by a conspirator — although the statute as written does not require one, and courts have generally not imposed one. *Compare* 18 U.S.C. § 371 (expressly requiring an overt act). **[INFERENCE]** The safer course is to plead an overt act; a block, a written policy, or a staff instruction is an overt act, and pleading one costs nothing.
4. **Deprivation of the right** — for a substantive conspiracy, the object need not be completed, but the conspiracy must be aimed at the deprivation.

### D. "Injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate"

**[INFERENCE]** The verbs are broad by design, and "oppress" is the one that fits an exclusion from a public forum. To "oppress" a person in the exercise of a right is to burden or suppress that exercise through the weight of official power. Blocking a resident from the official's forum, durably and by name, is the exercise of official power to suppress speech — the dictionary sense of oppression, and the statutory sense.

The defense will argue that these verbs require physical force. The text does not say so; the neighboring statute, § 245, does say "by force or threat of force" expressly, which shows that Congress knew how to require force when it wanted to. **[OBSERVED]** (Section 245's force requirement is precisely why it is not the right charge here; see Part XI.) **[INFERENCE]** A court that read a force requirement into § 241 would render the word "oppress" surplusage and would contradict the settled understanding that the statute reaches non-physical interference with constitutional rights.

### E. Conspiracy proof

**[INFERENCE]** Agreement is proved circumstantially, and a social-media moderation operation generates an unusual volume of circumstantial proof: shared block lists, shared log-in credentials, group chats, ticketing systems, task-assignment messages ("keep an eye on this account," "she's back"), moderators acting in sequence on the same targets, and the uniformity of the blocking criteria across accounts. None of that requires a signed agreement; it requires only that the conspirators acted together with a common purpose, which is what juries infer from coordinated conduct.

## X. The supporting counts

### A. 18 U.S.C. § 2 — principals

> "Whoever commits an offense against the United States or aids, abets, counsels, commands, induces or procures its commission, is punishable as a principal."

18 U.S.C. § 2(a). **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** The communications staffer who operates the block list, the deputy who drafts the "do not engage" instruction, and the contractor who runs the account are principals under § 2(a), not accessories. That matters because the official whose name is on the account is often the least involved person in the room.

### B. 18 U.S.C. § 595 — interference by administrative employees

> "Whoever, being a person employed in any administrative position by the United States ... uses his official authority for the purpose of interfering with, or affecting, the nomination or the election of any candidate for the office of President, Vice President, Presidential elector, Member of the Senate, Member of the House of Representatives ... shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than one year, or both."

18 U.S.C. § 595. **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** This is a narrow statute, and the narrowness is in the text. It reaches an administrative employee acting "in connection with any activity which is financed in whole or in part by loans or grants made by the United States," and it requires that the official authority be used "for the purpose of interfering with, or affecting, the nomination or the election" of a federal candidate. **[OBSERVED]** Three elements, each of which must be proved: federally financed activity, use of official authority, and electoral purpose. Where the blocking is ordinary irritation at criticism, no element beyond the first is present. Where officials purge opponents from an official channel during a campaign, all three can be. The count should be charged only on facts showing electoral purpose, because charging § 595 on a non-electoral motive would be overreach and would invite dismissal of the whole instrument.

### C. 18 U.S.C. § 2071 — concealment or destruction of records

> "Whoever willfully and unlawfully conceals, removes, mutilates, obliterates, or destroys, or attempts to do so, or, with intent to do so takes and carries away any record, proceeding, map, book, paper, document, or other thing, filed or deposited with any clerk or officer of any court of the United States, or in any public office, or with any judicial or public officer of the United States, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than three years, or both."

18 U.S.C. § 2071(a). **[OBSERVED]** Subsection (b), which applies to a person "having the custody of any such record," adds a forfeiture of office and disqualification from holding any office under the United States. **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** This count applies to deletion, not to blocking as such: a block hides the blocker's view of the target, but does not necessarily erase the target's comments from the record. Where the official deletes critical comments, or where the account's "hide reply" function removes them from the public record while the agency's records schedule requires their preservation, § 2071 is available. The disqualification clause in subsection (b) is the most severe collateral consequence in the entire statutory scheme, and it is the reason this count should be charged deliberately rather than cumulatively.

### D. 18 U.S.C. § 1519 — destruction in contemplation of a federal matter

> "Whoever knowingly alters, destroys, mutilates, conceals, covers up, falsifies, or makes a false entry in any record, document, or tangible object with the intent to impede, obstruct, or influence the investigation or proper administration of any matter within the jurisdiction of any department or agency of the United States ... shall be fined under this title, imprisoned not more than 20 years, or both."

18 U.S.C. § 1519. **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** This is the most defensible records count in the scheme, because it requires no pending proceeding. It reaches destruction intended to impede "any matter within the jurisdiction of any department or agency" — which includes the agency's own oversight of its social-media records. Where an official deletes a critical reply after a records request, an inspector-general inquiry, or a press inquiry that will predictably produce one, § 1519 fits and § 1512 may not.

### E. 18 U.S.C. § 1512(c)(1) — concealment in an official proceeding

> "Whoever corruptly— (1) alters, destroys, mutilates, or conceals a record, document, or other object, or attempts to do so, with the intent to impair the object's integrity or availability for use in an official proceeding ... shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than 20 years, or both."

18 U.S.C. § 1512(c)(1). **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** This count is conditional and fact-specific: it applies where comments are deleted *after* an investigation, audit, or congressional inquiry has begun, with the intent to keep them out of that proceeding. It is not a general records count, and it should not be charged absent proof of a pending proceeding and a corrupt purpose. Nor may the catch-all in (c)(2) be used to widen it: the Supreme Court has held that "the 'otherwise' provision of §1512(c)(2) is limited by the list of specific criminal violations that precede it in (c)(1)." *Fischer v. United States*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024). **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** The practical ranking among the records counts is therefore: § 1519 first (no pending matter required, twenty years), § 2071 second (three years, plus disqualification), § 1512(c)(1) last (pending proceeding and corrupt purpose).

## XI. Theories considered and not charged

A prosecutor's credibility depends as much on what he declines to charge as on what he charges. The following were considered and rejected for the reasons stated.

| Statute | Why not |
|---|---|
| 18 U.S.C. § 245 (federally protected activities) | Requires injury or interference "by force or threat of force." Blocking is not force. A threat-based variant is chargeable only on facts showing threats. |
| 18 U.S.C. § 249 (hate crimes) | Requires bodily injury and a bias motivation tied to race, religion, disability, sexual orientation, gender, or gender identity. Viewpoint is not within the statute. |
| 18 U.S.C. § 1505 (obstruction of agency proceedings) | Requires a "pending proceeding" or congressional inquiry. Blocking a constituent is not obstruction of one. |
| 18 U.S.C. § 598 (coercion by relief appropriations) | Requires use of relief or public-works appropriation to interfere with the right to vote. Not a speech statute. |
| 18 U.S.C. § 1030 (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act) | The official's use of his own account is authorized access. No unauthorized-access theory fits. |
| 18 U.S.C. § 876 (mailing threatening communications) | No threat element in blocking. |
| 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) as a *criminal* vehicle | Section 1985 is civil. No criminal counterpart reaches viewpoint. |

**[INFERENCE]** The table is not a concession that the conduct is lawful. It is a demonstration that the conduct is reached by the two oldest and most general civil-rights statutes in the Code — §§ 241 and 242 — which is exactly how Congress designed them to work.

## XII. Venue, limitations, and sentencing

**Venue.** 18 U.S.C. § 3237(a) provides that an offense "begun in one district and completed in another, or committed in more than one district, may be inquired of and prosecuted in any district in which such offense was begun, continued, or completed." **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** A block is begun where the official or staff acts and completed where the resident is silenced; both districts are proper, and the district of the resident is usually the better venue for a jury.

**Limitations.** 18 U.S.C. § 3282(a) requires indictment "within five years next after such offense shall have been committed" for non-capital offenses. **[OBSERVED]** **[INFERENCE]** A block is a continuing deprivation for as long as it lasts, so the limitations period should not begin to run until the block is lifted — but that is an argument, not a certainty, and stale conduct should be charged only with a continuing-offense theory pleaded in the indictment.

**Sentencing.** The Statutory Index assigns §§ 241 and 242 to U.S.S.G. § 2H1.1 (Offenses Involving Individual Rights). **[INFERENCE]** That guideline contains a specific offense characteristic for offenses committed under color of law, which will apply in every count here; counsel should compute the range from the current manual rather than from any figure quoted in a memorandum.

## XIII. Model charging language

**[INFERENCE]** The following is offered as pleading architecture, not as a finished indictment.

**Count One — 18 U.S.C. § 242.** On or about [date], in the [District], the defendant, [name], while acting under color of the laws of the United States, namely [organic statute / delegation order / agency regulation], willfully did deprive [victim], a person in the [District], of a right secured by the Constitution of the United States, namely the right to be free from viewpoint discrimination in a public forum, by blocking [victim] from [account] because of [victim]'s expressed political views, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 242.

**Count Two — 18 U.S.C. § 241.** From on or about [date] through [date], in the [District], the defendants, [names], did conspire to injure and oppress [victims] in the free exercise and enjoyment of a right secured by the Constitution of the United States, and because of their having exercised that right, namely the right to petition the government and to speak on matters of public concern, and in furtherance of the conspiracy the defendants [overt acts], in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 241.

**Count Three — 18 U.S.C. § 595.** On or about [date], the defendant, being a person employed in an administrative position by the United States, did use his official authority for the purpose of affecting the election of a candidate for [office], in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 595.

**Count Four — 18 U.S.C. § 2071.** On or about [date], the defendant, having custody of records of the United States, did willfully and unlawfully conceal, remove, and destroy records, namely [describe], in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 2071.

**[INFERENCE]** Four counts, in that order, track the theory of the case: the completed deprivation, the agreement that made it systematic, the electoral purpose where it exists, and the cover-up where it occurred.

## XIV. Immunities and affirmative defenses

### A. Speech or Debate

The Constitution provides that Senators and Representatives "shall in all Cases, except Treason, Felony and Breach of the Peace, be privileged from Arrest during their Attendance at the Session of their respective Houses, and in going to and returning from the same; and for any Speech or Debate in either House, they shall not be questioned in any other Place." U.S. Const. art. I, § 6, cl. 1. **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** The Clause protects legislative acts — voting, committee work, investigations, and the preparation of legislation. It does not protect a Member's press releases, newsletters, or constituent communications, which the Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished from legislative activity. A block administered by a communications staffer, in response to criticism, is not a legislative act; it is the operation of an office's public-facing channel. The Clause therefore does not bar a § 242 charge against a Member, though it will generate substantial motion practice and should be anticipated in the charging memorandum.

### B. Presidential immunity

*Trump v. United States*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), held that a former President is "entitled to absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for actions within his conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority," and "at least presumptive immunity from prosecution for all his official acts," with "[n]o immunity for unofficial acts." **[OBSERVED]** The presumption may be rebutted, and "[i]t is the Government's burden to rebut the presumption of immunity." **[OBSERVED]**

**[INFERENCE]** Three points for the charging decision. **First**, the immunity is personal to the President; it does not extend to Cabinet officers, agency heads, staff, or Members. The overwhelming majority of chargeable conduct in this area belongs to officials who have no such immunity. **Second**, for the President's own account, the threshold question is whether blocking critics is an "official act" at all. The Court's analysis turns on whether the conduct falls within a source of constitutional or statutory authority; there is no constitutional power to censor constituents, and the better view is that viewpoint-based blocking of private citizens is outside the official function — or, if inside its outer perimeter, that the presumption is rebutted because prosecuting it poses no danger of "intrusion on the authority and functions of the Executive Branch." **[OBSERVED]** (quoting the Court's rebuttal standard). **Third**, a prosecution of a sitting President raises separation-of-powers questions the Department must resolve at the highest level; that is a policy judgment, not a legal one, and this memorandum does not make it.

### C. Qualified immunity

Qualified immunity is a defense to damages, not to prospective relief, and it is unavailable where the right was clearly established. **[INFERENCE]** After *Lindke*, the right is clearly established for any official whose account is used for official business, and after *Knight* and *Davison* it has been clearly established in the Second and Fourth Circuits since 2019. The defense is therefore weakest where the government needs it most.

### D. The remaining defenses

| Defense | Answer |
|---|---|
| "The account is personal" | *Lindke*'s two-part test; official indicia; page-wide blocking reaches official posts |
| "This is government speech" | The forum is the interactive space, not the official's posts; *Knight*, 928 F.3d at 239 |
| "Blocking is not exclusion" | *Knight*: blocked users cannot view, reply, repost, or like; workarounds do not cure |
| "The resident can speak elsewhere" | *Knight*: "Neither does the fact that the Individual Plaintiffs can post messages elsewhere" |
| "We moderate everyone" | Prove it: the block list, the enforcement log, and the comparison set |
| "No injury" | Right to receive; nominal damages; declaratory relief |
| "Moot because the official left office" | Capable of repetition yet evading review; voluntary cessation; continuing practice |
| "No clearly established law" | *Knight* (2019), *Davison* (2019), *Lindke* (2024) |
| "Political question" | The claim is that a specific person was excluded for a specific reason; that is adjudicable |

## XV. Proving the case

### A. Preservation

The first act in any investigation should be a preservation letter to the official, the agency, and the platform. The relevant material is: the block list (many platforms expose it publicly), the account's audit log, staff communications about the target, the agency's social-media policy, the records schedule covering the account, and any retention or deletion instructions.

### B. The evidentiary core

**[INFERENCE]** Six categories of proof will decide most cases:

1. **The account record.** Archived posts, the biography, the linked agency page, and press releases that point to the account.
2. **The blocking record.** Who was blocked, when, by whom, and after what interaction.
3. **The comparison set.** Who was *not* blocked despite comparable conduct — the single most powerful proof of viewpoint selectivity.
4. **The internal record.** Emails, chats, tickets, and instructions. This is where "willfully" lives.
5. **The policy record.** A written policy that is content-neutral is a defense; the absence of one is proof.
6. **The notice record.** Prior litigation, press coverage, counsel's warnings, and internal legal review.

### C. Expert proof

**[INFERENCE]** Platform mechanics are not self-evident to a jury. An expert on social-media architecture can establish what a block does, what a "restrict" does, whether a blocked user can see posts while logged out, and whether a given account's replies were visible to third parties. That testimony converts the technical act into the constitutional injury.

### D. Statistical proof

**[INFERENCE]** Where the block list is large, a comparison of blocked versus unblocked accounts — by the content of their replies, their stated politics, and the timing of their blocks relative to their posts — can establish viewpoint selectivity as a matter of inference. Courts accept statistical proof of discriminatory pattern in civil-rights cases; there is no reason it should be unavailable here.

## XVI. What the courts have actually done

A theory is only as good as its record, so this section reports the record. The figures below come from a coding of every social-media blocking suit retrievable in the reviewer's CourtListener replica — twenty-two suits across thirty-eight clusters and twenty-nine dockets, filed between 2017 and 2026, of which eighteen have a recorded disposition. **[OBSERVED]** The coding is not a census of the world; it is a census of what the corpus holds, and it is reported that way.

| Outcome | Count | Cases |
|---|---|---|
| Merits judgment for the plaintiff (injunction and/or declaratory relief) | 5 | *Knight* (later vacated), *Davison*, *Garnier*, *Felts*, *Czosnyka* |
| Survived dismissal, no final merits ruling | 5 | *Windom*, *Attwood*, *Pinkhasov*, *Robinson* (remand), *Lindke* (remand) |
| Lost entirely or in operative part | 5 | *Raven*, *Taylor*, *Moulton*, *Blackwell*, *Gilley* |
| Settled or mediated | 2 | *Hikind*, *Biedermann* |
| Vacated as moot | 1 | *Knight*/*Biden* |

The table counts matters with a recorded disposition, and some matters appear in more than one row (the *Knight* litigation is both a merits win and a vacatur); the twenty-two suits are not twenty-two rows. **[INFERENCE]** Roughly one matter in four that reaches a merits ruling ends in judgment for the plaintiff. None ends in compensatory or punitive damages. The only money judgment located is one dollar in nominal damages, plus fees of $20,338.50 and costs of $3,215.52 in *Felts v. Green*. That is the realistic shape of the civil remedy: a declaration, an unblocking order while the official is still in office, nominal damages that survive resignation, and fees.

Three cases carry the most weight for a charging decision.

**The wins are real, and they survive appeal.** In *Garnier v. O'Connor-Ratcliff*, the Ninth Circuit held on remand from the Supreme Court that "O'Connor-Ratcliff's blocking of the Garniers on her social media accounts constituted state action under *Lindke*," and affirmed the declaratory and injunctive relief. **[OBSERVED]** That is the first post-*Lindke* appellate application, and it went the plaintiff's way.

**The damages claim died of immunity even there.** The district court granted "summary adjudication of Plaintiffs' damages claim because Defendants MOR and Zane are entitled to qualified immunity." **[OBSERVED]** The injunction lived; the money did not.

**A federal official has already won at the pleading stage.** *Raven v. Sajet* (D.D.C. 2024) dismissed a claim against the director of the Smithsonian's National Portrait Gallery because the plaintiff "has not plausibly alleged that Sajet purported to use government authority on her Twitter account." **[OBSERVED]** That is the single most important adverse data point in this memorandum, because it is the only federal-official merits ruling besides *Knight*, and it was a dismissal. **[INFERENCE]** The lesson is not that the theory fails against federal officials; it is that the theory fails against *thinly official* accounts. The *Lindke* showing must be built out of the agency's own documents before a complaint is filed.

## XVII. The honest weaknesses

A memorandum that overstated its case would be easy to defeat and would damage the credibility of everything else in it. The following are the real weaknesses, stated at full strength.

**First, *Knight* is vacated.** The Supreme Court vacated the Second Circuit's judgment as moot and remanded with instructions to dismiss. 593 U.S. ___ (2021). **[OBSERVED]** That means the holding binds no court, and a defendant will say so on the first page of the brief. The answer is that vacatur for mootness is not a ruling on the merits, that every court to address the question has followed *Knight*'s reasoning, and that *Lindke* adopted the state-action framework *Knight* assumed. But the attack has already landed in a district court: "Knight's precedential value has since been gutted by a Munsingwear vacatur." *Pinkhasov v. Vernikov*, No. 23-CV-3460 (E.D.N.Y. 2024). **[OBSERVED]** Any memorandum that cites *Knight* as authority without that concession is not credible.

**Second, the forum question is unsettled for federal accounts.** No court of appeals has squarely held that a federal agency's account is a designated public forum. The government will argue that agency accounts are government speech and that replies are a nonpublic forum. **[INFERENCE]** That argument may succeed in some circuits. It narrows the case but does not defeat it, because viewpoint discrimination is barred in every forum.

**Third, *Egbert* forecloses damages.** There is no honest way around it for a First Amendment claim. The civil case is a case for an injunction, a declaration, fees under a statute that permits them, and administrative relief.

**Fourth, § 242's application to federal officers rests on a 1945 sentence and a 1981 circuit holding — not on a modern prosecution of expressive conduct.** The textual argument in Part VIII.C is backed by the Supreme Court's own reading of the statute: "He who acts under color of law may be a federal officer or a state officer. He may act under color of federal law or of state law." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 108 (1945), as quoted in *Hesse v. Town of Jackson*, 2008 WL 4222040 (D. Wyo. Sept. 15, 2008). **[OBSERVED]** And the Ninth Circuit rejected the state-law-only reading outright, affirming § 242 convictions of Border Patrol agents. *United States v. Otherson*, 637 F.2d 1276 (9th Cir. 1981). **[INFERENCE]** But no court has applied § 242 to a federal officer's *expressive* conduct, and *Otherson* involved physical abuse. The gap is real: a court could accept that federal officers are covered and still hold that viewpoint-based exclusion from a comment thread is not the kind of "deprivation" § 242 reaches. That risk should be disclosed to the charging official in writing.

**Fifth, willfulness is a genuine hurdle.** A staffer who blocks a foul-mouthed critic because she is foul-mouthed has not committed a crime, even if the block burdens speech. The line between irritation and viewpoint discrimination is a fact question, and juries sometimes believe the official.

**Sixth, mootness follows the official, and it is the default exit.** When an official leaves office the account passes and the block lapses. *Biden v. Knight* is the proof at the Supreme Court, and the Ninth Circuit dismissed the claim against trustee Zane on remand for exactly that reason. **[OBSERVED]** No case in the retrieved set has applied "capable of repetition, yet evading review" to rescue a blocking claim against a departed official. **[INFERENCE]** What survives is declaratory relief, nominal damages, and fees — *Felts* is the model — plus the criminal case, which the defendant's departure does not moot.

**Seventh, the only federal-official merits ruling went against the plaintiff.** *Raven v. Sajet* (D.D.C. 2024) dismissed a claim against a Smithsonian director for failure to plead that she "purported to use government authority" on the account. **[OBSERVED]** A federal blocking case must therefore be built on the agency's own communications records, not on the official's stature.

**Eighth, the APA may not be available at all.** A single block is a poor candidate for "final agency action" under § 704 and *Bennett*, and courts have not treated account moderation as reviewable. The equitable count against the officer is the sturdier route; the APA count is a fallback.

**Ninth, qualified immunity has already been granted for blocking.** *Dingwell v. Cossette* (D. Conn. 2020) granted it because the right was not clearly established in 2015. **[OBSERVED]** That is pre-*Knight* conduct, but it shows how a court will reason about older facts.

**Tenth, prosecutorial discretion is real.** A United States Attorney who charges a misdemeanor against a Cabinet officer for blocking a constituent will be accused of criminalizing politics. That accusation is an argument about discretion, not about law, and this memorandum's purpose is to show that the law permits the charge — not that every case warrants it.

## XVIII. The structural argument: why the law must reach this conduct

The doctrinal case is strong; the structural case is stronger.

A government that can silence individual critics while claiming to speak for all citizens has stopped being accountable to the citizens it claims to represent. The First Amendment's guarantee is not a guarantee that the government will listen. It is a guarantee that the government will not use its power to prevent citizens from speaking to one another about it. *Knight* put the point in a sentence: the government "is not permitted to 'amplify' favored speech by banning or burdening viewpoints with which it disagrees." 928 F.3d at 238. **[OBSERVED]**

The statutes Congress enacted to enforce that guarantee are old, general, and unimpaired. Sections 241 and 242 were written for precisely this: the use of official power to injure a person in the exercise of a constitutional right. They have been applied to sheriffs, police officers, prison guards, and, in the Ehrlichman prosecution, to officials of the United States. **[INFERENCE]** They apply here. The forum is new; the wrong is not.

---

## XIX. Recommendation

**[INFERENCE]** On the analysis above, the following course is recommended:

1. **Investigate** the account, the block list, the staff, and the policy. Preservation letters first.
2. **Charge § 242** where a specific resident was blocked for a specific viewpoint and the official had notice that the account was an official forum.
3. **Charge § 241** where two or more persons agreed on the practice, and charge the staff under § 2.
4. **Charge § 595** only on evidence of electoral purpose.
5. **Charge § 1519** where records were destroyed with intent to impede a federal matter, and **§ 2071** only where the destruction is of a record "filed or deposited" in a public office; treat § 2071(b)'s disqualification clause as the reason to charge it deliberately.
6. **Pursue civil relief in parallel**: injunction and declaration against the officer; § 1983 against state co-actors; § 1985(3) where animus fits; Hatch Act complaint; records referral to the Attorney General under 44 U.S.C. § 3106.
7. **Disclose the weaknesses** in the charging memorandum, in writing, before the indictment is presented.

The conduct described in this memorandum is not a policy disagreement. It is the exercise of governmental power to punish speech, and the law has a name for it.
