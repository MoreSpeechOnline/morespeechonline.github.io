# Blocking as Censorship — Summary of the Argument

*Summary of the full memorandum, "Blocking as Censorship: Civil and Criminal Liability of Federal Officials Who Exclude Residents from Social-Media Accounts Used for Official Business" (21 pp.).*

*Markers: the full memorandum tags each proposition as observed in retrieved text, reported from a decision whose text is not in the research corpus, or inferred. This summary keeps that discipline in prose.*

**When a federal official blocks a resident from a social-media account used for official business, the official violates the First Amendment and, where the elements are proved, commits a federal crime.**

## The conduct

A **covered account** is one an official operates, directly or through staff, in the character of the office: the title in the biography, the agency's website linking to it, staff drafting and moderating, policy announced through it, constituent service conducted on it. **Blocking** is the platform act that stops a named person from viewing, replying to, reposting, or interacting — total, durable, and status-based, unlike muting or a content rule. The violation requires that the block be **because of** the person's protected speech: proved directly (what the official said about the target), circumstantially (timing and the pattern of who was blocked), or structurally (no content-neutral rule explains the selection).

## The constitutional violation

**It is government action.** *Lindke v. Freed*, 601 U.S. 187, 198 (2024), requires that the official "possessed actual authority to speak on the State's behalf" and "purported to exercise that authority." A federal account branded with the office and run by official staff satisfies both — and the Court removed the obvious escape: "An official cannot insulate government business from scrutiny by conducting it on a personal page." *Id.* at 202 n.2. The federal government has no state-action gap: the First Amendment addresses Congress in terms, and a federal officer is the government. The "personal account" defense survives only where the account is genuinely personal, and it cannot rescue a page-wide block on a mixed account — as the Court itself observed.

**It is viewpoint discrimination.** *Knight First Amendment Institute v. Trump*, 928 F.3d 226, 236 (2d Cir. 2019), held that an account used "as an official vehicle for governance," with interactive features "accessible to the public without limitation," is a public forum, and that "[i]f the Account is a forum—public or otherwise—viewpoint discrimination is not permitted." The Supreme Court vacated that judgment as moot, so it binds no one — a district court has already said that "*Knight*'s precedential value has since been gutted by a *Munsingwear* vacatur" (*Pinkhasov v. Vernikov*, E.D.N.Y. 2024). But no court has rejected its reasoning, *Lindke* adopted the framework it assumed, and in 2025 the Ninth Circuit applied that framework and again sustained a blocking claim against a school board official (*Garnier v. O'Connor-Ratcliff*). The rule it applies is older than the case: viewpoint discrimination is barred in every forum, including a nonpublic one. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Viewpoint discrimination is, in the Court's words, "poison to a free society." *Iancu v. Brunetti*, 588 U.S. 388 (2019).

**The government-speech defense fails.** The official's own posts may be government speech; the replies are not. *Knight* turned on "his supervision of the interactive features of the Account," not on his tweets. The government "is not permitted to 'amplify' favored speech by banning or burdening viewpoints with which it disagrees." 928 F.3d at 238.

**The injury is not cured by workarounds.** Logging out, searching, or posting elsewhere does not cure the violation. *Id.* at 234. Blocking burdens the right to speak, the right to receive, and the right to petition — in the one channel the government opened for all three.

## Civil liability

- **Damages against a federal officer are foreclosed.** *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482, 484 (2022): "*Bivens* does not extend to create causes of action for ... First Amendment retaliation claims." Any memorandum that promised damages would be wrong.
- **Prospective relief is available.** 5 U.S.C. § 702 waives sovereign immunity for relief "other than money damages," and § 706(2)(B) requires a court to set aside agency action "contrary to constitutional right." An injunction should name the officer and successors personally, as § 702 requires.
- **Section 1983 reaches state and local actors, and federal officers acting jointly with them.** It does not reach federal officers acting under federal law — its text names only the States, Territories, and the District.
- **Section 1985(3) reaches conspiracies**, but requires class-based animus; viewpoint alone is not a class. *Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic*, 506 U.S. 263 (1993). Plead it in the alternative.
- **Administrative and records regimes carry real weight, but no private right of action.** A Hatch Act complaint lies only where the object is electoral — the Act excludes the President and Vice President and defines political activity by reference to a party or candidate. And the Federal Records Act is enforced through the agency head, the Archivist, and the Attorney General: *Kissinger* holds no private right "can be implied," and *Armstrong* preserves only a suit to compel the § 3106 referral, which the Archivist "shall" make — with notice to Congress — when the agency head will not. 44 U.S.C. § 3106(b).

## Criminal liability

**18 U.S.C. § 242** punishes whoever, "under color of any law," "willfully subjects any person ... to the deprivation of any rights ... secured ... by the Constitution." Four elements: official action, willfulness, a federal right, a person in a State or District. The base offense is a one-year misdemeanor.

The critical textual point: § 242 says "any law," while § 1983 says "of any State or Territory or the District of Columbia." Congress limited the civil statute and did not limit the criminal one. The Supreme Court read it that way: "He who acts under color of law may be a federal officer or a state officer. He may act under color of federal law or of state law." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 108 (1945). The Ninth Circuit affirmed § 242 convictions of Border Patrol agents and rejected the state-law-only reading as finding no support in the statutory text. *United States v. Otherson*, 637 F.2d 1276, 1278–79 (9th Cir. 1981).

Willfulness is the honest battleground — but it is a fact about purpose, proved by timing, target selection, statements about the target, persistence after notice, and the absence of any neutral criterion. After *Knight* (2019) and *Lindke* (2024), no official can claim the line was unknown.

**18 U.S.C. § 241** is the better charge where two or more persons acted together. It reaches conspiracies to "injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate" a person in a federal right "or because of his having so exercised the same" — a retaliation clause written into the statute. It requires no state action, so staff and consultants are covered, and it carries ten years rather than one.

**Supporting counts**: § 2 (staff as principals); § 1519 (destroying records with intent to impede a federal matter — twenty years, no pending proceeding required); § 2071 (concealment or destruction of records in a public office — three years, plus forfeiture of office and disqualification); § 595 (use of official authority to affect a federal election — one year, only on proof of electoral purpose and federally financed activity); § 1512(c)(1) (concealment in a *pending* proceeding, and only that — *Fischer* forecloses the catch-all).

**Not charged, and why**: § 245 requires force or threat of force; § 249 requires bodily injury and bias motive; § 1505 requires a pending proceeding; § 1030 requires unauthorized access. Naming them shows the theory is bounded.

## Defenses, and the weaknesses

Speech or Debate does not cover a communications staffer's block. Presidential immunity under *Trump v. United States* (2024) is personal to the President — absolute for core constitutional powers, presumptive for other official acts, and rebuttable — and it does not shield subordinates; for the President's own account the official-act question is contestable and the presumption rebuttable.

The real weaknesses: *Knight* is vacated; no appellate court has held a federal agency account to be a designated public forum; *Egbert* closes the damages path; no court has applied § 242 to *expressive* conduct by a federal officer (the coverage of federal officers themselves is settled by *Screws* and *Otherson*); willfulness can fail before a jury; and mootness follows an official out of office.

## What the record shows

Of the twenty-two blocking suits retrievable in the reviewer's case corpus (2017–2026), eighteen have a recorded disposition: five ended in a merits judgment for the plaintiff, five survived dismissal, five lost, two settled, and one was vacated as moot. No compensatory or punitive damages award exists; the only money judgment is $1.00 in nominal damages plus $20,338.50 in fees (*Felts v. Green*). The winning defense is never "the First Amendment does not apply" — it is "this account was personal." And a federal official has already won at the pleading stage on exactly that ground (*Raven v. Sajet*, D.D.C. 2024).

## Bottom line

The forum is new; the wrong is not. Sections 241 and 242 were written for the use of official power to injure a person in the exercise of a constitutional right. A federal official who blocks a resident from the official channel because of what the resident said has done exactly that — and the law has a name for it.
